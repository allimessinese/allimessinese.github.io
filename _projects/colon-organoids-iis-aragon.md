## What I Learned

The most disorienting part of this visit was realising how much the actual science diverges from the version you write in a research proposal. The proposal I had written with Lieke van Dongen was careful, structured, and grounded in literature. Standing in a surgical suite watching a revascularized pig liver scaffold being handled under blue light was something else entirely: the organ glowed faintly pink, the surgical team moved with practised efficiency, and the problem of immune rejection felt viscerally real rather than abstractly important.

The visit also clarified why regenerative medicine is hard to accelerate. The timelines for getting from a decellularized scaffold to something clinically useful are long not because the science is immature, but because every step, the decellularization, the recellularisation, the vascularisation, the immune management, has to be solved to a high standard simultaneously. Watching the full spectrum of the lab's work, from pig fetus liver to lung to colon, made that interdependence concrete.

The third thing the visit clarified was about conditions. The same protocol, run in two different labs by two different scientists, rarely produces identical results. Not because anyone made an error, but because conditions that feel controlled are not fully controlled: the timing, the exact concentration of a reagent, the particular habits of the person running it. None of that gets written into the paper. Regenerative medicine is especially sensitive to this because the material is biological and the variance is real. What I took from it was a new respect for knowledge transfer as its own discipline. Tracking everything precisely and making the implicit explicit is not administrative work. It is the infrastructure that lets the science move.

## The Context

During my Minor in Regenerative Medicine at TU Delft, one module required students to design an original research proposal. My proposal, co-written with **Lieke van Dongen**, was titled **"Pig Liver Scaffold Modification to Enhance Compatibility with Human Recipients."**

The core problem: every day, around 17 people die waiting for an organ transplant. Decellularized animal scaffolds offer a route toward transplantable organs that bypass the donor shortage, but the remaining extracellular matrix components can trigger immune rejection in human recipients. Our proposal focused on modifying those ECM components, through targeted gene editing and biochemical treatment, to reduce immunogenicity without compromising scaffold architecture.

Writing the proposal gave me command of the literature. The visit to Zaragoza gave me command of the reality.

## The Lab

Pedro Miguel Baptista's group at the **Centro de Investigación Biomédical de Aragón (CIBA)**, part of **IIS Aragón**, is one of the leading labs in whole-organ engineering. They work across liver, lung, and colon, using decellularization as the common scaffold-building technique and layering recellularisation and vascularisation strategies on top.

The lab's output spans animal models, scale-up for human-sized organs, and translational questions around immune compatibility, all the same problems I had just been writing about from first principles.

**Revascularized pig liver scaffold transplantation.** The centrepiece of the visit was observing a transplantation procedure involving a revascularized pig liver scaffold, one of the most advanced demonstrations of the field in practice. A pig liver had been decellularized to leave only its extracellular matrix and vascular tree, then repopulated with cells and the vascular network restored before transplant. The organ was translucent and structured, its vasculature visible, glowing under the surgical light. Seeing it handled in a sterile environment, by a team who had run this protocol many times, was a different category of understanding than reading about it.

**Pig fetus liver decellularization.** I also witnessed the decellularization of pig fetus livers, the process by which detergents are perfused through the organ's vascular tree to strip away cellular material while preserving the collagen and laminin scaffold beneath. The result is a ghostly, semi-transparent structure that retains the organ's full architecture without any of its original cells.

**Colon organoid isolation and development.** I contributed to the isolation and early-stage development of colon organoids: three-dimensional tissue models derived from intestinal stem cells that self-organise into crypt-like structures mimicking the colon's architecture. These sit at the opposite end of the complexity spectrum from whole-organ scaffolds, but the questions they address overlap: how do you build functional tissue, and how do you validate that it behaves like the real thing.

## The Research Proposal

**Pig Liver Scaffold Modification to Enhance Compatibility with Human Recipients**
*Allegra Messinese and Lieke van Dongen, TU Delft Minor in Regenerative Medicine, 2023*

The proposal centred on the immunogenicity problem: decellularized pig liver scaffolds retain ECM components, including alpha-gal epitopes and porcine-specific proteins, that trigger rejection in human recipients. We proposed two complementary approaches: (1) targeted gene editing to knock out porcine immunogenic sequences at the source before decellularization, and (2) post-decellularization biochemical treatment to further neutralise residual antigens. The goal was to preserve scaffold mechanical integrity and vascular patency while reducing the recipient immune response to a manageable level.
