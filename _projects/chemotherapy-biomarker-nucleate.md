## What I Learned

ChemoMicrobiome was my first experience owning commercialisation for a life sciences startup from the inside. It sits at exactly the intersection I want to work in: hard science with a clear clinical need, a regulatory path that is complex but navigable, and a market where the value proposition is measurable in dollars saved and lives less disrupted.

The most important thing I learned was how much the commercial case depends on translation. The science is compelling on its own terms: four microbial genes, metagenomic sequencing, 95% accuracy in predicting treatment discontinuation. But the people who make purchasing decisions in oncology clinics are not reading microbiome research papers. Getting from the bench to a reimbursed diagnostic requires a completely different kind of argument, built around cost savings, regulatory precedent, and clinical workflow. I built that argument.

Customer discovery also changed how I think about market sizing. The instinct is to start with the total addressable market and work down. The more useful direction is to start with the specific patient population where the problem is most acute and the solution is most defensible, then expand from there. For ChemoMicrobiome, that meant colon cancer patients on oral fluoropyrimidines: a well-defined, high-risk group where the evidence is strongest and the entry path is clearest.

## The Problem

Chemotherapy toxicity is not a side effect footnote. It is a clinical crisis that costs the US healthcare system over \$10 billion annually and forces many patients to abandon treatment altogether. The conventional approach is a single standardised regimen applied to everyone: same drug, same dose, same schedule, regardless of whether an individual patient is likely to tolerate it or not.

The result is predictable. Patients who could have benefited from treatment stop early because the physical toll becomes unsurvivable. Patients who could have been managed on a lower dose end up hospitalised. The treatment designed to fight the cancer becomes its own threat.

The gap is not a lack of effective drugs. It is a lack of tools to predict, in advance, which patients are at risk.

## The Technology

ChemoMicrobiome has built a microbiome profiling diagnostic designed to give clinicians exactly that predictive capability before treatment begins.

The mechanism is grounded in decades of microbiome and pharmacology research. When a patient submits a stool sample, the company extracts DNA and performs metagenomic sequencing to profile the composition of the gut microbiome. A validated model then uses that profile to predict the probability of drug toxicity across treatment options, allowing the patient and their oncologist to select a regimen that maximises efficacy while minimising risk.

The predictive model is unusually specific: using just four microbial genes, it identifies patients who need to be discontinued from treatment with **95% accuracy**, with high sensitivity and specificity validated across multiple clinical studies. The test is noninvasive, low-cost, and fits into standard pre-treatment workflows without requiring new clinical infrastructure.

## The Market

The timing is right for several reasons. Precision medicine is becoming standard practice in oncology: the field has already accepted genomic profiling as a routine step in treatment planning, and microbiome profiling is the logical next layer. Research from institutions including UCSF has established that gut microbiota influence drug absorption and response. The regulatory and reimbursement environment is increasingly receptive to companion diagnostics that reduce downstream costs.

The immediate target population is the approximately **100,000 patients most at risk from fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy**, concentrated in oncology clinics and research hospitals that see high volumes of complex cases and tend to be early adopters of diagnostic innovation. Cost savings are estimated at \$5,000 to \$10,000 per patient per year from avoided ER visits and hospitalisations, with a projected \$1 billion saved across global healthcare systems by 2030.

Beyond the diagnostic, aggregated microbiome data creates a second value layer: pharmaceutical companies can use it to develop more targeted drugs and reduce trial-and-error prescribing, accelerating the path from research to approved therapy.

## My Role: Head of Commercialisation

I joined ChemoMicrobiome as Head of Commercialisation through the Nucleate Activator program, responsible for building the commercial case and defining the path to market.

**Customer discovery.** I engaged oncologists, hospital administrators, and biopharma stakeholders to map where the pain is sharpest, what decision-making looks like in practice, and what evidence would be required to change clinical behaviour. The conversations shaped the product positioning more than any desk research could have.

**Competitive landscape.** I analysed existing predictive tools, adjacent diagnostics, and emerging technologies to identify where ChemoMicrobiome is differentiated and where the risks are. The core finding: no current solution offers passive, pre-treatment toxicity prediction at this level of specificity and at a cost that makes broad adoption viable.

**Regulatory pathway.** I mapped the FDA and CMS coverage routes to understand what validation is required and in what sequence. Early FDA engagement is part of the go-to-market strategy precisely because it de-risks the timeline and creates a reimbursement anchor.

**Value proposition and GTM.** The go-to-market entry point is oncology clinics and research hospitals, running pilot studies that generate both clinical validation and institutional buy-in. Payer strategy runs in parallel, positioning the diagnostic as a cost-reduction tool rather than an add-on expense.

## NSF I-Corps

ChemoMicrobiome also participated in the **NSF I-Corps program**, the National Science Foundation's commercialisation training initiative designed to help research teams rigorously test whether a scientific innovation has a viable path to market.

I-Corps is built around a single discipline: customer discovery. Teams conduct a structured series of interviews with potential customers, partners, regulators, and payers to stress-test their assumptions before committing resources to a specific commercial direction. The process is deliberately uncomfortable. Every assumption about who needs the product, how they would buy it, and what would make them change behaviour has to survive contact with real people before it is treated as a fact.

For ChemoMicrobiome, participation in I-Corps strengthened the commercial thesis considerably. The interviews confirmed the clinical pain around chemotherapy toxicity, surfaced how oncologists actually make treatment decisions, and sharpened the reimbursement framing. It also forced a more precise answer to the question of who the primary customer actually is: not the patient, not the hospital system in aggregate, but the oncologist who needs a tool that fits into a workflow they already trust.

## About the Nucleate Activator

Nucleate is a student-led organisation that supports the translation of academic science into biotech companies. The **Activator program** is its flagship venture-building initiative, designed to take early-stage life sciences technologies from laboratory to market-readiness.

The program brings together scientists and business builders through a mutual-matching process, then provides strategy support, industry mentorship, legal and clinical consulting, and hands-on workshops over the course of the cohort. It culminates in a Demo Day pitch event in front of investors and industry leaders.

Nucleate does not take equity. The program has supported over 183 companies, which have collectively raised \$550 million and created 800 jobs.

For me, the Activator was the structure that made it possible to do serious commercial work on a pre-incorporation startup: real mentorship, real accountability, and access to the kind of expert network that would otherwise take years to build independently.
