## What I Learned

The most lasting thing this placement gave me was a calibration. Before it, research was an abstraction: something that happened in buildings with important names, conducted by people who had spent decades accumulating expertise. After two weeks at L.I.T.A., it became something specific: a set of procedures, each with its own timescale, its own failure modes, and its own requirement for precision. Watching a histological slide take days to prepare from a single thin section of rat cerebellum adjusted my sense of what scientific progress actually looks like.

The second thing I took away was more personal. The team was studying neuromelanin in the context of Parkinson's Disease. The connection between a dark pigment in the brain, the cells it protects, and the degeneration that eventually defines the disease is not obvious until you see it being investigated up close. That specificity, that gap between what is known and what remains to be understood, was what made me want to work in biomedical science rather than just study it.

The third thing I took away wasn't about the science at all. The PI I spent time with was doing work that mattered. The questions she was pursuing were the kind that could genuinely change how Parkinson's is understood and treated. And she was struggling to continue. Not because the science had stalled. Because the funding had. Watching someone with that level of expertise and that quality of work face a constraint that had nothing to do with the research itself was the first time I understood that the gap between a discovery and the people it could help isn't only scientific. It's also structural. Someone has to build the bridge.

## The Lab

**L.I.T.A.** (Interdisciplinary Laboratory of Advanced Technologies) is a unit within CNR, the Italian National Research Council, based in Segrate outside Milan. CNR is Italy's principal public research institution, spanning genetics, computer science, nanotechnology, materials science, and biomedical research across dozens of specialised institutes.

The group I joined fell under the neuroscience division, where researchers were investigating the relationship between neuromelanin and the progression of Parkinson's Disease.

## The Science

Parkinson's Disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder whose most recognisable symptom is tremor, caused by the gradual loss of neurons in the brain. The team's specific focus was **neuromelanin**: a dark pigment present in the brain that plays a protective role, absorbing potentially harmful substances from surrounding tissue and releasing them in non-toxic form.

The central research question concerned the relationship between neuromelanin synthesis and accumulation on one side and cellular degeneration on the other. Understanding how neuromelanin behaves across the course of the disease requires being able to observe and analyse it directly, which is why histology and molecular biology were the core tools of the lab.

## The Experiments

### Histological slide preparation

The first experiment was histological. The goal was to prepare rat cerebellum samples for microscopic analysis, which requires reducing tissue to sections thin enough for light to pass through and for cellular structures to become visible.

The process takes several days and proceeds in stages: sectioning the cerebellum into thin slices, staining the tissue using a sequence of alcohols at different concentrations to highlight its structures, then examining the finished slides under different types of microscope. Each stage depends on the precision of the one before. An error in sectioning or staining produces a slide that cannot be read.

### Agarose gel electrophoresis

The second experiment was molecular. The objective was to separate DNA into its nucleic acid components using agarose gel electrophoresis, a technique that exploits the negative charge carried by DNA and RNA molecules. When an electric field is applied across the gel, fragments migrate toward the positive electrode at rates proportional to their size, producing a ladder of distinct bands made visible under UV light.

I performed this on both my own saliva and on a mouse tissue sample, which made the difference between the two genome profiles directly observable on the same gel.

## My Role

This placement was part of the **Alternanza Scuola-Lavoro** programme, the Italian compulsory school-work transition scheme, carried out in the final year of my secondary education. I joined the research team for two weeks in June 2019, approximately 60 hours of lab time at L.I.T.A., Via Fratelli Cervi 93, Segrate. The contact researcher was Luca Pasqualetto.

It was my first time in a professional research environment. The experience made the connection between laboratory science and actual disease research concrete in a way that classroom biology never had.
